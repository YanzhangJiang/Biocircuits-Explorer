Introduction to Reaction Order Polyhedra and the Biocircuits Explorer workbench

A short, textbook-style tour of the theory behind the polyhedron, the objects you see on screen, and the recipes that take you from a sketched binding network to a design conclusion.

1What this wiki is

Biocircuits Explorer is a workbench for designing and analysing equilibrium binding networks through the lens of Reaction Order Polyhedra (ROP). The product page on the homepage shows the capabilities at a glance; this wiki goes one level deeper. It explains the mathematics the app is computing, the language it uses for the objects it draws on the canvas, and the practical recipes that turn a question about a circuit into an answer.

Read it linearly the first time. Once you have built a few networks, each chapter section is meant to stand on its own as a reference.

Who this is for

New users of the web or macOS app who want to know what the regime graphs, SISO paths and polyhedra are actually showing them — not just how to click the buttons. No prior background in algebraic geometry or chemical reaction network theory is assumed; basic equilibrium thermodynamics (mass action, conserved totals) is.

2Theory of Reaction Order Polyhedra

2.1What is a Reaction Order Polyhedron?

A Reaction Order Polyhedron is a geometric object that summarizes everything an equilibrium binding network can do qualitatively, before you commit to specific numerical values for the binding constants or the conserved totals.

The input to an ROP analysis is a binding network:

  • A finite set of base species \( X_{1}, \ldots, X_{n} \).
  • A finite set of complexes — stoichiometric combinations such as \(AB\), \(AAB\), \(ABCD\). A complex is an integer vector \( a \in \mathbb{N}^{d} \setminus \{0\}\) with size \( |a|_{1} = \sum_{i} a_{i} \le \mu \), where \(\mu\) is the maximum allowed complex size.
  • A finite set of reversible association reactions \( C_{a} + C_{b} \rightleftharpoons C_{a+b} \) with equilibrium constants.
  • A stoichiometry matrix \(N\) (rows are reactions) and a conservation-law matrix \(L\) whose rows encode the conserved totals \( q_{i} = \sum_{j} L_{ij}\,x_{j} \).
  • A choice of which totals act as inputs \(u\), which are fixed conserved totals \(q\), and which complex concentration plays the role of the output \(y\).

What comes out is not a single equilibrium curve but a polyhedron in log-concentration / log-parameter space. Each point of the polyhedron is a dominance mode — a snapshot of which species “wins” inside each conserved pool. Each vertex corresponds to a complete asymptotic regime; each face or edge is a transition between regimes.

The geometry is polyhedral because the equilibrium equations

\[ N \log x \;=\; \log k, \qquad L\,x \;=\; q \]

are non-linear in concentrations but piecewise log-linear in the asymptotic limit. As soon as one term dominates inside each total, every species concentration collapses to a single monomial in the free variables, and the output becomes a linear function of the log-parameters. The polyhedron is the map of which monomial wins where. A curve describes one parameter setting; the polyhedron describes the whole parameter space at once.

2.2Why dominance, why log-log?

At chemical equilibrium, every species concentration is determined by mass action plus conservation. Each conserved total is a sum of monomials in the free concentrations of the base species:

\[ q_{i} \;=\; \sum_{j \,:\, L_{ij} > 0} L_{ij}\, x_{j}. \]

For the simplest example \( A + B \rightleftharpoons AB \) with \( K = [AB] / ([A]\,[B]) \), the total of \(A\) is a sum of two monomials in the free concentrations \([A]\) and \([B]\):

\[ q_{A} \;=\; [A] + [AB] \;=\; [A] + K\,[A]\,[B]. \]

The central observation behind ROP is that at most parameter combinations, one of those monomials is much larger than the others. Pick any generic point in log-concentration space, and one term wins each sum. When you change parameters, you cross thin boundaries where the winning term flips. Inside one regime, the relationships among concentrations are pure power laws.

In log-log coordinates the picture becomes clean. If the output \(y\) and input \(u\) satisfy \( y \asymp C\,u^{\rho} \) in some regime, then \( \log y = \rho \log u + \mathrm{const.} \) The slope is exactly the reaction order:

\[ \rho \;=\; \frac{\partial \log y}{\partial \log u}. \]

Two facts make this useful:

  1. Within a regime, \( \rho \) is an integer (or a simple rational arising from stoichiometry), because the dominating monomial is a single product of integer powers.
  2. Across a one-dimensional input scan, the response is piecewise linear in log-log: a sequence of straight segments with integer slopes, joined at turning points where one monomial overtakes another.

The whole qualitative behavior of a circuit is therefore captured by a finite symbolic program of reaction orders:

\[ b \;=\; (\rho_{1}, \rho_{2}, \ldots, \rho_{L}), \quad\text{for example}\quad 0 \to -1 \to +1 \to 0 \to -2. \]

Each entry is a slope; each transition is a turning point on the polyhedron. For multi-input / multi-output slices the analogous object is a sequence of gain matrices \( G_{\ell} = \partial \log y / \partial \log u \).

2.3The objects on the polyhedron

Biocircuits Explorer uses the vocabulary established in the ROP papers. Each object below is something the app can compute or draw on the canvas.

Reaction-order matrix
The Jacobian of \(\log x\) with respect to \(\log(q, k)\). Its entries are the local reaction orders. Vertices, faces and the polyhedron itself are organized around how this matrix changes.
Assignment
A choice of one dominating species per conserved total: in pool \(i\), species \(j\) holds (almost) all the mass.
Assignment polytope  \(\mathrm{AssignP}(L)\)
The convex hull of every legal assignment — a coarse outer envelope, the Cartesian product of one simplex per total.
Dominance regime
An assignment that is actually realizable by some \( x \in \mathbb{R}^{n}_{>0} \). Some assignments are spurious; only the realizable ones are real regimes.
Dominance polytope  \(\mathrm{DomP}(L)\)
The convex hull of the dominance regimes. This is the tight polyhedral envelope of the dominance modes \( A(x) = \Lambda_{Lx}^{-1} L\, \Lambda_{x} \). Every vertex is an achievable regime.
Vertices · faces · edges
Vertices = complete asymptotic regimes (one winner per total). Faces = partial regimes with ties between several species. Edges = one-step transitions where a tie resolves. Walking around the polytope is walking through the regimes a real circuit visits as parameters change.
Supports
The set of monomial terms that survive in a given regime. Supports change from vertex to vertex.
Input/output projection
Choosing a scanned input \(u\) and an output \(y\) projects the polyhedron onto the \((\log u, \log y)\) plane. The image is the SISO regime structure.
SISO path
The sequence of vertices the system visits as the scanned input is swept from \(0\) to \(\infty\). The reaction-order program \( b = (\rho_{1}, \ldots, \rho_{L}) \) reads off the slopes along that path.
Asymptotic vs. singular regimes
Inside a vertex \( \rho \) is a finite integer. On certain faces it can be infinite or undefined (vertical jumps in log-log). The app keeps the distinction — singular tokens \(\pm \infty\) and NaN are not collapsed into a finite reaction order.
Capability periodic table
A two-coordinate index \((d, \mu)\) — the number of base species and the maximum complex size — under which each cell is an entire design space. Cells carry a capability envelope: mechanisms realized, strength extrema, robustness extrema, minimal witness networks, and certificates of un-reachability.
Sign words
Run-length-compressed three-state programs over \(\{-, 0, +\}\). The zero state is real (saturation / invariance) and is never deleted by compression. Mechanism classes include activation from zero \(0 \to +\), settling to zero \(+ \to 0\), direct reversal \(+ \to -\), and zero-mediated reversal \(+ \to 0 \to -\).

2.4What the polyhedron tells you about a circuit

Once the polyhedron is built and a SISO (or MIMO) projection is chosen, several practically useful objects fall out:

  • Response orders. The slopes \(\rho_{\ell}\) along the SISO path are the local Hill-like exponents. A regime with \(\rho \approx 0\) is saturated; \(\rho \approx 1\) is linear; \(\rho > \mu\) is ultrasensitive relative to the maximum complex size.
  • Sensitive vs. saturated regions. The polyhedron shows exactly where in log-parameter space each lives, before any numerical sweep.
  • Switching loci / turning points. Faces of the polyhedron — projected to the input axis — give the inputs at which qualitative behavior changes. A boundary has the affine log-space form \[ a_{u}\,\log u + a_{q}^{\top}\!\log q + a_{K}^{\top}\!\log K + c = 0, \] so a turning point sits at \[ \log u^{\ast} \;=\; -\,\frac{a_{q}^{\top}\!\log q + a_{K}^{\top}\!\log K + c}{a_{u}}. \] Whether a switch is controlled by binding constants, by conserved totals, or mixed, is a built-in mechanistic classification.
  • Upper-bound reachability. Conservation gives \( x_{a} \le U_{a}(q) = \min_{\,i:\,a_{i}>0}\, q_{i}/a_{i} \). Whether the output's vertex sits at this bound tells you whether the circuit can be driven to its stoichiometric ceiling.
  • Robust regions. A program may be strong but fragile, weak but robust, or both. The polyhedron is the geometric object on which the volume-fraction robustness score (the R-index) is computed.
  • Achievable behavior families. Different mechanisms can produce the same regime graph and sign word; the periodic table groups them into classes (activation, repression, settling to zero, zero-mediated reversal, multi-turn programs, MIMO rank expansion). The atlas workflow searches this family space.

2.5A worked example: \(A + B \rightleftharpoons AB\)

Take the simplest non-trivial binding network, with equilibrium constant

\[ K \;=\; \frac{[AB]}{[A]\,[B]}, \qquad q_{A} = [A] + [AB], \qquad q_{B} = [B] + [AB]. \]

Scan \(q_{A}\) as the input \(u\), hold \(q_{B}\) fixed, take \(y = [AB]\) as the output. The conservation matrix is

\[ L \;=\; \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \]

so each row of the dominance-mode matrix \(A(x)\) is a one-simplex: for the \(q_{A}\) row, mass sits somewhere between \(A\) and \(AB\); for the \(q_{B}\) row, between \(B\) and \(AB\). The four assignments are the four corners of the resulting square — and for this network all four are achievable, so the dominance polytope is the square. The vertices read:

  • \((A, B)\): both species mostly free. \([AB] \approx K\,q_{A}\,q_{B}\), so \(\rho = 1\).
  • \((AB, B)\): \(A\) is mostly bound, \(B\) mostly free. \([AB] \approx q_{A}\), so \(\rho = 1\) but the output is now hitting the \(A\) ceiling.
  • \((A, AB)\): \(B\) mostly bound, \(A\) mostly free. \([AB] \approx q_{B}\), constant in \(u\), so \(\rho = 0\) — saturation.
  • \((AB, AB)\): both totals collapse on the same complex; the limiting species switches between \(A\) and \(B\).

As \(u = q_{A}\) is increased, a SISO scan typically walks \( (A,B) \to (AB,B) \to (A,AB) \), reading off the reaction-order program

\[ \rho \,:\, 1 \to 1 \to 0, \]

or, after run-length sign compression, a clean settling-to-zero response \( S(b) \,:\, + \to 0 \). Each vertex of the square is a regime; each edge is a turning point; the whole square is the dominance polytope for this circuit. Increasing \(\mu\) or \(d\) inflates the square into higher-dimensional polyhedra with richer vertex structure — which is exactly the design space the periodic table is built to chart.

3The canvas, in one minute

3.1Nodes, ports, wires

The web app opens to a blank canvas. You build an analysis by placing nodes and wiring their ports:

  • Add a node. Click Add Node in the toolbar and choose from the categorized menu (Utilities, Input, Parameters, Process, Results).
  • Connect ports. Each node has typed input sockets on the left and output sockets on the right. Click an output, drag to a matching input.
  • Run. Most nodes have a Run button. The toolbar's Run Connected executes the whole connected subgraph in dependency order; Cloud Compute sends heavy work to a remote backend.
  • Save. Save Workspace exports the whole canvas — nodes, parameters, wires — as portable JSON.

3.2Node families

The same five families recur:

Input
reaction-network for hand-entered reactions; network-id-definition to load a compressed network ID.
Process
model-builder turns reactions into a symbolic model (\(N\), \(L\), session ID). atlas-builder assembles a library of networks from an atlas spec.
Parameters
siso-params, scan-1d-params, scan-2d-params, rop-cloud-params, rop-poly-params, fret-params, atlas-spec, atlas-query-config.
Results
model-summary, vertices-table, regime-graph, siso-result, qk-poly-result, scan-1d-result, scan-2d-result, rop-cloud-result, rop-poly-result, fret-result, atlas-query-result, atlas-inverse-result.
Utilities
markdown-note for free-form notes you keep next to a workspace.

4Recipes — what to do, step by step

Each recipe below is a self-contained workflow. The flow line at the top of each one is the wire diagram you should build on the canvas.

4.1Build a model and inspect its regimes

Start here. This is the prerequisite for every other recipe.

reaction-network → model-builder → model-summary + vertices-table + regime-graph

  1. Enter your reactions

    Add a reaction-network node. For each reaction, type a line like E + S <-> C_ES and a dissociation constant Kd. Add as many lines as you need.

  2. Build the model

    Wire reaction-network → model-builder. Press Run. The builder fixes the symbolic order of species, complexes, totals and constants and returns a session ID downstream nodes will reuse.

  3. Inspect the model

    Wire model-builder → model-summary to see \(n\), \(d\), \(r\), the species list, the totals, the binding-constant symbols, and the matrices \(N\) and \(L\).

  4. List the vertices

    Add vertices-table off the same model. Each row is a vertex of the polyhedron: its permutation of species, type (asymptotic vs. non-asymptotic), singular / invertible status, and nullity.

  5. See the regime graph

    Add regime-graph. The dropdown lets you switch between qK-neighbor (regimes joined by single parameter transitions) and SISO mode.

Reading the output

The vertices table is the discrete index of the polyhedron. The regime graph is its 1-skeleton. Together they tell you how rich the qualitative behavior of your network can be — before you have chosen a single number.

4.2Trace a SISO behavior path

Use this when you want to know how the circuit responds to changing one specific input.

model-builder → siso-params → siso-result → qk-poly-result

  1. Choose input, output and bounds

    In siso-params pick the \(qK\) change variable (the swept input), the target species \(x\) (the output), the path scope (feasible, robust, or all), the minimum volume fraction, and the log\(_{10}\) bounds for the scan.

  2. Enumerate behavior families

    Run siso-result. The node returns a ranked list of SISO paths — each path is a reaction-order program \( b = (\rho_{1}, \ldots, \rho_{L}) \) with a feasibility volume.

  3. Visualise the chosen path

    Click a path in the list. Wire to qk-poly-result to see the projected polyhedron in \(qK\)-space for that path.

Reading the slopes

The sequence of integers in a SISO path is exactly the local log-log slope at each step. If you see 0 → 1 → 0 you have a clean activation that saturates; 1 → 0 → −1 is a non-monotone response with a peak. The signs alone, run-length compressed, are the “sign word” the periodic table indexes.

4.3Run a parameter scan

When you want a numerical response curve, not just the symbolic path.

model-builder → scan-1d-params → scan-1d-result

  1. Pick a parameter and a range

    In scan-1d-params choose a \(qK\) variable, the log range and number of points, and type an output expression — for example 2*C_ES + E.

  2. Run and read

    scan-1d-result draws the output expression versus the scanned variable. Crosshairs and value readouts feed downstream nodes.

  3. Go 2D when needed

    Swap to scan-2d-params / scan-2d-result for a heatmap across two parameters simultaneously.

4.4Visualize the ROP polyhedron and the point cloud

The polyhedron itself is one node; the point cloud sampled across parameter space is another. Together they let you spot robust regions.

model-builder → rop-poly-params → rop-poly-result
reaction-network → rop-cloud-params → rop-cloud-result

  1. Configure the polyhedron view

    In rop-poly-params choose 2D or 3D, and pair each axis with a species \(x\) and a \(qK\) parameter. The inner sample count controls how the interior is filled in.

  2. Sample the cloud

    rop-cloud-params picks the sampling mode (x-space or qK-based), the number of samples, the target species and the log-bounds. The result node plots the cloud overlaid on the polyhedron.

  3. Look for the dense regions

    Vertices with a dense surrounding cloud are robust regimes — the circuit lands there for a large fraction of plausible parameter combinations. Sparse vertices are fragile.

4.5Atlas search and inverse design

The atlas workflow goes the other direction: given a behavior you want, find networks that achieve it.

atlas-spec → atlas-builder + atlas-query-config → atlas-query-result / atlas-inverse-result

  1. Specify the search space

    atlas-spec has four tabs: Basic (source label, SQLite path, profile), Behavior (path scope, min volume), Enumeration (pairwise binding, reaction counts) and Explicit (JSON list of networks).

  2. Preview the library

    Wire to atlas-builder and click Build Preview — this generates a slice of the atlas so you can verify the spec before committing to a full build.

  3. Define the design goal

    atlas-query-config lets you state the goal: an I/O pair (e.g. tA -> AB), a motif label, an exact behavior, a witness path, or forbidden regimes. Pick a ranking mode — minimal_first or robustness_first.

  4. Run forward or inverse

    atlas-query-result returns the ranked match list. atlas-inverse-result additionally runs the build + query loop with negative-example refinement and returns candidates with evidence.

Heads up

The canvas builder is intended for previews and small scratch atlases. For full-scale library construction use the CLI scripts in webapp/build_atlas.jl and friends, then point the query nodes at the resulting SQLite file.

5What it’s for — design applications

ROP analysis pays off in three broad situations:

  1. Reading off Hill-like exponents without curve-fitting. The integer slopes on the SISO path are the local response orders before you choose any constants. You can spot whether an ultrasensitive response is achievable at all by checking whether any regime has \( \rho > \mu \).
  2. Finding robust operating regimes. The point-cloud density at each vertex is a robustness score: a vertex visited by a large fraction of parameter samples is a regime the circuit will land in across many cellular contexts.
  3. Inverse design. Given a target signal-processing behavior (a sign word, a target peak height, a robust settle-to-zero), the atlas tools enumerate the binding networks that realize it, ranked by minimality or robustness.

Less obvious, but equally useful: ROP analysis gives a clean way to reason about why a circuit fails. If your target response is not in the polyhedron of any network in the periodic-table cell \((d, \mu)\) you searched, the failure is structural, not numerical — no choice of binding constants will fix it. The app reports such certificates explicitly.

6How to read the outputs

A good ROP session is not just a stack of plots. The app is exposing several layers of evidence: a symbolic model, a finite list of regimes, an adjacency structure, asymptotic response orders, and numerical checks. This section gives a practical reading order.

6.1Regime table checklist

Start with model-summary and vertices-table. They tell you whether the network you sketched became the network you intended to analyze.

Output field What it means What to check
species, x_sym The species order used by the backend. Make sure complexes such as AAB or C_ES are named consistently.
q_sym, K_sym The conserved totals and binding constants that form the \(qK\) coordinate system. These are the only variables a SISO scan can sweep directly.
N The stoichiometry matrix for reaction constraints. Unexpected rank or row count usually points to a malformed reaction list.
L The conservation-law matrix. Each row should match a conserved pool you can explain chemically.
Vertex type Whether a regime is asymptotic, singular, invertible, or degenerate. Finite response orders come from regular asymptotic vertices; singular entries need separate interpretation.
Nullity The local degree of freedom left after the active constraints are imposed. High nullity means the regime is underdetermined or lives on a larger face, not an isolated corner.

If the model summary already looks wrong, do not proceed to SISO. Fix the reaction list first. A beautiful polyhedron for the wrong conserved pools is still the wrong answer.

6.2SISO interpretation checklist

A SISO result should be read at three levels: the exact slope program, the sign-compressed behavior, and the geometry that supports it.

Pattern Interpretation Design question
0 → + Activation from a flat baseline. Can the output turn on only after the input crosses a boundary?
+ → 0 Response that saturates. Is the output ceiling set by a conserved total or by a binding constant?
+ → - Direct reversal: increasing the input eventually represses the output. Which complex steals the limiting species at the reversal?
+ → 0 → - Zero-mediated reversal with a plateau. Is the plateau robust enough to be a useful operating window?
+ → 0 → + Two-stage activation separated by saturation. Are there two independent binding thresholds?
NaN or ±∞ Singular or vertical response in the projected coordinates. Is this a meaningful jump, or did the chosen output vanish in that regime?

The sign word is a summary, not the whole story. Two paths can share a sign word but differ in strength, vertex sequence, transition control, and robustness. Use the full slope list and the projected polyhedron before treating two behaviors as equivalent.

6.3Numerical scans versus asymptotics

The ROP polyhedron is an asymptotic object: it predicts which monomial dominates in wide log-parameter regions. Parameter scans are finite numerical evaluations. They should agree in the large-scale shape, but they answer different questions.

  • Use SISO paths to learn which qualitative behaviors are structurally possible.
  • Use parameter scans to check the finite constants you actually plan to use.
  • Use ROP cloud to estimate which regimes occupy substantial parameter volume.
  • Use atlas search when the current network cannot realize the behavior and you need candidate alternatives.

A useful rhythm

Build the model, read the vertices, choose an input and output, enumerate SISO paths, inspect the projected polyhedron, then run a numerical scan through the same coordinates. When these disagree, the disagreement is information: it usually points to a finite-size effect, a narrow transition, or an output expression that is not aligned with the asymptotic coordinate.

7Node reference

The canvas is a dataflow system. A node is useful when you know what it consumes, what it produces, and which backend endpoint it normally calls.

Model construction

reaction-network stores reactions and dissociation constants. model-builder calls /api/build_model and emits the session used by most downstream analysis nodes.

Regime structure

model-summary, vertices-table, and regime-graph expose the symbolic model, vertices, and adjacency graph via /api/find_vertices and /api/build_graph.

SISO analysis

siso-params defines the \(qK\) coordinate and output. siso-result calls /api/siso_paths and related endpoints; qk-poly-result renders selected path geometry.

Scans and surfaces

scan-1d-result, scan-2d-result, fret-result, rop-cloud-result, and rop-poly-result turn a session into numerical curves, heatmaps, sampled clouds, and polyhedron projections.

Atlas workflows

atlas-spec, atlas-builder, atlas-query-config, atlas-query-result, and atlas-inverse-result cover build, query, and inverse-design workflows.

Workspace notes

markdown-note has no backend dependency. It is there so a saved workspace can carry assumptions, candidate mechanisms, or why a particular path was accepted or rejected.

7.1Common wiring patterns

Question Minimum node chain Typical next step
Did my reaction list parse correctly? reaction-network → model-builder → model-summary Add vertices-table after the summary looks right.
What regimes exist? model-builder → vertices-table Add regime-graph to inspect transitions.
How does one input affect one output? model-builder → siso-params → siso-result Select a path and view it with qk-poly-result.
What curve will I see for one finite parameter set? model-builder → scan-1d-params → scan-1d-result Compare the curve to the SISO slope program.
Which network can realize my target behavior? atlas-spec → atlas-query-config → atlas-query-result Run atlas-inverse-result for build + query refinement.

8Atlas and inverse design

The atlas layer turns many individual ROP analyses into a searchable design library. This is where the project stops being a single-model workbench and becomes a map of capability space.

8.1The four atlas objects

Atlas spec
The recipe for what to analyze: explicit networks, enumerated search spaces, behavior configuration, source labels, and optional SQLite persistence.
Atlas
A single build result. It usually contains network entries, behavior slices, and family buckets.
Atlas library
A stable aggregate assembled from one or more atlas builds. Use this when you want a versionable knowledge base.
Atlas SQLite
A persisted form for large or incremental libraries. Query nodes and CLI scripts can read from this directly.

8.2How to formulate a query

Good atlas queries are specific about both behavior and evidence. Start with an input/output pair, then add only the constraints that matter.

  • Motif queries ask for broad classes such as activation, repression, reversal, or settling.
  • Exact behavior queries ask for a specific slope list or sign word.
  • Witness queries require a concrete path through vertices, not only a matching summary label.
  • Forbidden-regime queries exclude circuits that visit undesirable intermediates.
  • Robustness queries rank or filter by volume fraction rather than by existence alone.

8.3What inverse design returns

Inverse design is not just “search harder.” It combines build, query, negative knowledge, and candidate refinement. A useful result should tell you:

  • which networks matched;
  • which target kind was used (motif, exact behavior, witness, or support);
  • whether a new atlas build or merge was performed;
  • which candidates were rejected and why;
  • whether the match is structurally exact or only a soft support match.

A failed inverse-design run can still be useful. If a whole \((d,\mu)\) cell fails an exact support screen, that is evidence that the desired behavior needs either more base species, larger complexes, or a different modeling assumption.

8.4Canvas versus CLI

Use the canvas while shaping the question. Use the CLI once the question is stable and the run becomes large. 

julia --project=webapp webapp/build_atlas.jl spec.json atlas.json
julia --project=webapp webapp/query_atlas.jl atlas.json query.json result.json
julia --project=webapp webapp/run_inverse_design.jl spec.json inverse_result.json

The SQLite route is the right choice when you expect to reuse the library across many sessions or merge incremental builds over time.

9AI Import — turn a paper into a model

Reading a paper, transcribing every reaction by hand and looking up every Kd is the slow path. The ai-import node (in the red Input family) does it for you: it reads source material you supply, asks a language model to extract every distinct equilibrium binding network the source describes, and drops one populated analysis chain on the canvas per network found.

Privacy posture

The API key never leaves your browser. The host app does not proxy or store keys server-side — requests go directly from the page to the chosen provider. Tick “Remember for browser session” only if you trust the machine you are sitting at.

9.1What the node does

The agent has a closed contract. It is allowed to:

  • Read pasted text, a PDF attachment, or a Jupyter .ipynb (only source cells; outputs are stripped locally before the file is sent).
  • Emit one or more networks, each carrying a list of reversible binding reactions, a Kd in molar, and a confidence label.
  • Pick recommended downstream analyses from a fixed allow-list. Anything else is silently ignored.
  • Add a warnings list for every approximation, unit conversion, or discarded model.

When you click Insert chain on a network card, the node drops a reaction-network with the extracted reactions, wires it into a model-builder, and adds one result node per recommended analysis. Each spawned node is a normal canvas node — edit reactions, change Kd values, rerun, save.

9.2Choosing a provider and supplying a key

Two providers are supported, both speaking the Anthropic Messages API shape:

Provider Models Endpoint Document attachments
Anthropic Claude Opus 4.7, Sonnet 4.6, Haiku 4.5 api.anthropic.com/v1/messages Yes (PDF)
DeepSeek V4 Pro, V4 Flash (Anthropic-compatible) api.deepseek.com/anthropic/v1/messages Text and notebook only

Pick a model from the dropdown or type a custom model ID. The provider is detected from the model name (anything starting with claude goes to Anthropic, anything starting with deepseek goes to DeepSeek). The API key field is a password input; if you leave “Remember for browser session” unchecked, the key is forgotten as soon as you close the tab.

9.3Inputs: text, PDF, notebook

Three input tabs, mutually exclusive in spirit but combinable:

Text
Paste a section, supplementary methods, abstract, anything relevant. Useful when you only want a few specific reactions out of a long paper.
PDF
Upload a paper. The whole PDF is sent as one document content block. Expect higher token usage than the text path. Only available on Anthropic models.
Notebook
Upload a .ipynb. The browser parses it locally and sends only source cells (markdown + code). Output cells, base64 images and large data blobs never leave your machine.

9.4Reading the results and the auto-spawned chains

A finished run renders three things in the node body: a short paper summary, one card per extracted network, and a warnings list. Each network card carries an Insert chain button that materialises this on the canvas:

reaction-network → model-builder → [recommended results]

The recommended-results slot expands per network: a model-summary always appears, plus zero or more of vertices-table, regime-graph, siso-result, scan-1d-result, scan-2d-result, rop-cloud-result, rop-poly-result, fret-result. The agent is constrained to this allow-list; anything else it tries to recommend is dropped.

9.5Honesty rules — and what gets discarded

The system prompt explicitly tells the model not to fabricate. Common honest-extraction patterns:

  • Kd not stated. A placeholder 1e-3 M is emitted, confidence is marked low, and the warning names the reaction. Topology survives; you must edit the value.
  • Only Ka given. Agent emits \( K_{d} = 1 / K_{a} \) and notes the inversion in warnings.
  • Only kon / koff. Agent emits \( K_{d} = k_{\mathrm{off}} / k_{\mathrm{on}} \) and notes it.
  • Hill functions, phenomenological dose-responses, catalytic turnover. Discarded with a warning. The agent will not invent a binding scheme.
  • Unit conversion. nM / µM / mM are converted to M with a warning.

A run that returns zero networks plus a summary explaining why is the correct outcome when the source does not contain extractable equilibrium binding chemistry. Returning zero is treated as honest, not as a failure.

10Accounts and sign-in

On a public deployment the cloud backend requires a signed-in user. On a local or on-prem deployment the sign-in button stays hidden and every analysis runs against the in-process Julia backend with no account at all.

10.1When you need to sign in

You need an account only for cloud-backed work:

  • Submitting Cloud Compute jobs (atlas build, inverse design).
  • Hitting per-user quotas — the daily submission counter is keyed by your Cognito user.
  • Persisting result artifacts in the cloud backend's S3 prefix.

Local SISO analyses, regime graphs, point clouds, parameter scans, model summaries — everything in §4 — work without an account. The toolbar's Sign in button only appears when the backend reports auth is enabled.

10.2The sign-in flow

Authentication uses AWS Cognito Hosted UI with the OAuth Authorization Code + PKCE flow:

  1. Click Sign in in the toolbar

    The button is hidden if Cognito is not configured. When it is, the SPA fetches /api/auth/config to learn the Cognito domain and public app-client ID.

  2. Go through Cognito Hosted UI

    The browser is redirected to …amazoncognito.com/oauth2/authorize. Sign up or sign in there with email + password. Email verification and optional MFA are handled by Cognito.

  3. Bounce through /auth-callback.html

    Cognito redirects back to /auth-callback.html with an authorization code. The SPA exchanges the code for ID / access / refresh tokens using the PKCE verifier it stashed before redirect.

  4. Return to the app

    Tokens are written to localStorage and the SPA navigates back to the page that initiated sign-in. From this point every cloud job submission attaches Authorization: Bearer <id_token>.

ID tokens auto-refresh five minutes before expiry using the refresh token. Signing out clears local tokens and visits Cognito's /logout endpoint so the next sign-in lands on the Hosted UI fresh.

10.3Same flow on macOS

The Swift macOS app runs the same auth.js inside a WKWebView. Tokens live in the WebView's own localStorage. There is no native Keychain integration today — a future Swift-side change could switch to ASWebAuthenticationSession + Keychain without touching the web codebase.

The Cognito app client must register the macOS WebView's loopback callback (default http://127.0.0.1:18088/auth-callback.html) alongside the web origin. See deploy/AWS_BATCH.md §2.2 for the full callback-URL list.

10.4Per-user quotas

When the deployment opts in with --with-quota-table, a DynamoDB counter is keyed by your Cognito sub. Every cloud job submission writes to it; the counter resets via a TTL field once per day.

The default daily limit is 50 jobs per user; the operator can raise or lower it with BIOCIRCUITS_EXPLORER_QUOTA_DAILY_LIMIT. Exceeding the limit returns HTTP 429 from the cloud backend and no Batch job is queued. Local analyses are not counted.

11Cloud Compute — running heavy jobs remotely

Two analyses can take minutes or hours: building a full atlas, and running inverse design over a large search cell. The Cloud Compute toggle redirects those jobs to a remote AWS Batch queue while keeping every other analysis on the in-process Julia backend.

11.1Toggling Cloud Compute

The toolbar has a Cloud Compute button that flips a session-wide flag, persisted in localStorage:

  • ON. Atlas builds and inverse-design runs are submitted as jobs to the cloud backend. The result node shows the job ID, a live status badge, and the link to the artifact when it finishes.
  • OFF. The same nodes run their work locally and block the canvas while doing so. Fine for small previews.

Toggling Cloud Compute has no effect on lightweight analyses (SISO paths, scans, polyhedron viewers). Those always run locally regardless of the flag.

11.2Which jobs run remotely

Endpoint Route when Cloud Compute is ON Route when OFF
build_atlas AWS Batch job Local Julia worker
run_inverse_design AWS Batch job Local Julia worker
build_model, find_vertices, siso_paths, parameter_scan_*, rop_*, fret_* Always local — the toggle is ignored.

11.3The deployment stack

On the operator side the cloud route uses four AWS surfaces. You do not need to know the details to use the app, but they matter when reading job logs or planning a deployment:

ECR
Stores the worker Docker image. It is the same image as the local backend, with a Batch entrypoint that reads input.json from S3 and writes status.json / result.json back.
S3
One prefix per job, holding input.json, status.json, and result.json. The frontend polls status.json and downloads result.json when the status becomes succeeded.
AWS Batch
Runs the worker image as a container in a managed compute environment. Default minvCpus=0 so the cluster scales to zero between jobs. The job definition is registered with an execution role so ECR image pulls and CloudWatch log delivery work on both EC2-launch and Fargate-launch environments.
DynamoDB (optional)
Per-user daily submission counter keyed by Cognito sub. Enabled with --with-quota-table in setup_aws_batch.sh. See §10.4 for the user-facing behaviour.

A signed Cognito ID token must accompany every POST /api/jobs request when Cognito is configured. The cloud backend rejects unsigned calls with HTTP 400 “Missing Authorization Bearer token”.

For operators

The full setup script, IAM policy and callback-URL list live in deploy/AWS_BATCH.md. It also covers the Cognito integration and the DynamoDB quota table.

11.4Watching a remote job

The result node renders the job lifecycle inline:

  • Job ID returned by Batch as soon as submission succeeds.
  • Status badge stepping through pending → submitted → running → succeeded / failed / cancelled.
  • Result artifact linked when the run succeeds. For atlas builds, the artifact is a SQLite file you can re-feed into an atlas-spec node on any future canvas session.
  • Error message from the worker when the run fails. Deeper logs live in CloudWatch under the Batch job's log group.

A job's full lifecycle — submission, queue, container start, run, result upload — is opaque from the canvas: you only see the status badge. If you need detail, the Batch console and the per-job S3 prefix are the right places to look.

12Troubleshooting and limits

Most confusing results come from one of three places: a reaction list that does not encode the intended chemistry, a stale model session, or an output expression that is not aligned with the chosen coordinate.

Symptom Likely cause What to do
Invalid session_id The backend restarted, the page was refreshed with stale nodes, or the session expired. Run model-builder again, then rerun downstream nodes.
Unexpected species or missing complex Reaction naming mismatch or malformed reaction syntax. Check model-summary before running any result node.
No SISO paths returned The selected \(qK\) coordinate/output pair has no feasible path under the current filters. Relax path scope or volume thresholds; then try a different output species.
Scan curve disagrees with expected sign word Finite constants are not deep enough in the asymptotic region, or the output expression mixes species. Widen the log range and compare with the projected polyhedron.
Atlas query finds too many networks The query describes only a broad motif. Add an exact behavior, witness, forbidden-regime, or robustness constraint.
Atlas query finds nothing The search cell, enumeration profile, or support condition is too restrictive. Try a larger \((d,\mu)\) cell or inspect the negative evidence.

12.1Modeling limits

The current workbench is built for equilibrium binding networks and conserved totals. It is not a general-purpose simulator for time-dependent kinetics, irreversible production/degradation, spatial transport, stochastic copy-number noise, or arbitrary ODE models. You can sometimes approximate those questions by choosing a steady-state binding subproblem, but that approximation should be stated explicitly.

The strongest answers are structural: possible / impossible behaviors, regime transitions, asymptotic slopes, and robustness in log-parameter space. Numerical scans are provided to connect that structure to finite parameter values, not to replace a full kinetic simulation when the biology demands one.
